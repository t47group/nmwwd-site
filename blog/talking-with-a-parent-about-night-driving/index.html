Talking with a parent about night driving

Nino Mihilli· October 2, 2026 ·3 min read

If you have noticed that a parent seems less comfortable driving at night, you are facing a conversation that most families handle badly — not from lack of care, but because the obvious approach almost never works.

Why the direct version fails

The instinct is to state the concern plainly: you should not be driving at night anymore.

It fails because of what the person hears, which is not a comment about driving. Driving is how they get to the doctor, the grocery store, church, and their friends. It is the difference between managing their own life and asking permission for it.

So a conversation you experienced as safety, they experienced as the beginning of losing independence — and the reasonable response to that is resistance, regardless of the merits.

Start smaller than you want to

The most common mistake is opening with the largest possible ask.

Night driving is a narrow, specific behavior. Suggesting fewer night trips, or avoiding one particular complicated interchange, is a modest request that can be accepted without conceding anything about the future.

Starting with "you should stop driving" makes every smaller step feel like the first move in a longer campaign — which, to be fair, it often is. Being honest with yourself about the actual concern matters here.

Be specific about what you noticed

"I am worried about your driving" is unfalsifiable and feels like a judgment about competence.

"I noticed on the way back from dinner that the freeway exit was hard to pick out" is a specific observation about a specific situation. It can be discussed, agreed with, or corrected. It invites a conversation rather than a defense.

Specificity also keeps the conversation about a manageable problem rather than about aging.

Solve the transportation problem first

This is the step that changes outcomes most, and it is usually skipped.

Much of the resistance is not about driving. It is about what happens when you cannot drive. If the conversation includes a concrete alternative — a standing arrangement for a weekly errand, a ride-share account already set up with someone who will help use it, a specific person who will drive on Thursdays — you are proposing a swap rather than a loss.

Arriving with the alternative already arranged does more than any argument.

Bring in a physician

Two of the biggest contributors to night-driving difficulty are medical and correctable: vision changes and medication effects.

An eye exam may find something addressable. A medication review may identify a prescription, or an interaction, that affects alertness or vision at night — something the person taking it frequently does not know.

A recommendation from a doctor also lands differently. Coming from an adult child it sounds like a judgment. From a physician it is medical advice, and it is usually more accurate than a family member's impression.

Talk about the specific conditions

Wrong-way crash research identifies elevated risk in a recognizable cluster of conditions: nighttime and dawn/dusk, unfamiliar roads, and drivers far from home. Those risks are not evenly spread, and neither is the reasonable response.

A familiar ten-minute daytime route is a different situation from an unfamiliar freeway interchange at 10pm. Treating them the same is what makes the conversation feel unfair, because it is.

Agreeing to avoid the highest-risk combination — unfamiliar roads after dark — is a real reduction in exposure that preserves nearly everything the person actually cares about.

What to avoid

Ambushing them with the whole family present. It feels like an intervention because it is one, and it produces defensiveness rather than agreement.

Taking keys without a conversation. It creates a conflict about autonomy that will outlast the driving question.

Making it about age. It is about vision, medication, and specific conditions. Those are addressable, and framing it that way is both kinder and more accurate.

Having it once. This is a series of conversations over months, not a single decision. Expecting resolution in one sitting sets everyone up to fail.

The reframe worth using

The goal is not fewer older drivers. It is fewer people in the situations where these crashes happen.

Almost everything that helps here — better lit interchanges, clearer signage, avoiding unfamiliar roads at night, an up-to-date eyeglass prescription — helps every driver. Framing it as a shared problem rather than a personal deficiency makes the conversation possible.

And if the answer is that the roads themselves are hard to read at night, that is not a failure of the driver. That is what road design and detection technology exist to fix.
