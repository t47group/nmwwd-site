One of the best things about living in the Valley is that winter is a short drive away. Thanksgiving weekend and the weeks after it bring a steady stream of Phoenix drivers up I-17 and other routes toward Flagstaff, the White Mountains, and the Grand Canyon, many of them looking for snow.

It is also a drive that can change completely in a few hours. Desert drivers who rarely see ice can find themselves on mountain highways in a storm, after dark, with heavy traffic and few places to stop.

## Check before you go

ADOT's consistent advice is that one of the best things a driver can do is check weather and road conditions before leaving. Current highway conditions are available at az511.gov or by calling 511.

ADOT also says that waiting out a storm is the safest and most efficient option. Its "Know Snow" guidance describes the safest choice as waiting until snow is no longer falling and plows have cleared the highways.

If a storm is forecast for the day you planned to drive, moving the trip by a day is often the best safety decision you can make.

## Prepare the vehicle

ADOT recommends checking your vehicle before heading into winter weather, including at a minimum the tires, heater, and windshield wipers. Its winter guidance also points to the battery, ignition and exhaust systems, thermostat, defroster, and brakes, along with working lights and turn signals.

ADOT also advises keeping your gas tank at least three-quarters full and carrying a fully charged phone. Traffic backups in the high country can last much longer than expected, especially on busy weekends.

## Pack a winter kit

ADOT's winter driving guidance lists items worth carrying when you drive into snowy or icy conditions, including:

- Warm clothing: gloves, scarves, caps, and extra socks
- Blankets
- Food and water
- Necessary prescription medications
- A first-aid kit
- A flashlight with extra batteries
- An ice scraper
- A small bag of sand or cat litter for traction
- A small folding shovel
- A tool kit and battery cables
- Safety flares

For a family trip, add whatever your passengers would need to stay warm and comfortable during a long wait in a stopped car.

## Driving in snow and ice

ADOT's core tips are simple: **slow down and leave extra room** behind the vehicle ahead. Stopping distances grow dramatically on snow and ice, and four-wheel drive does not help a vehicle stop.

**Give snowplows room.** ADOT advises against passing a plow that is clearing the highway until the driver pulls over to let traffic pass, and warns drivers never to assume a plow operator knows their vehicle is nearby.

**Do not park on the shoulder to play in the snow.** ADOT says highway shoulders are for emergencies only. Parked cars can be hit by other drivers, they block emergency vehicles and plows, and plows can throw snow and ice up to 30 feet off the roadway. ADOT has posted signs along US 180 northwest of Flagstaff warning drivers not to park on shoulders unless it is an emergency.

**Expect delays coming home.** ADOT has noted that the return trip to Flagstaff from snow play areas on US 180 can take 90 minutes or longer on busy days.

## Plan for daylight

Winter days are short, and the drive back down to the Valley often starts late in the afternoon after a day in the snow. That puts many drivers on I-17 after dark, tired, and in heavy traffic.

Darkness is one of the clearest risk factors for wrong-way crashes. ADOT reports that three out of four wrong-way crashes happen between 6 p.m. and 6 a.m. Leaving earlier, or staying the night, reduces your exposure.

If you do drive in the dark:

- Watch the signs at every interchange, especially at unfamiliar exits for gas and food.
- If you see headlights coming toward you on a divided highway, ADOT advises easing off the gas, steering away from the wrong-way driver when no vehicle is beside you, getting to a safe place, and calling 911.
- If an overhead sign warns of a wrong-way driver, move toward the nearest exit as soon as it is safe.

## Keep alcohol out of the plan

A cabin weekend or a holiday visit with family up north can include drinks. Make the plan for who drives before the trip starts, and do not let anyone drive mountain highways impaired. Snow, ice, darkness, and impairment are a combination nothing on the road can fully protect against.

## Enjoy the snow, then get home

Winter in Arizona's high country is worth the drive. A little preparation, a flexible schedule, and patience with the weather go a long way toward making sure the trip ends safely back home.
