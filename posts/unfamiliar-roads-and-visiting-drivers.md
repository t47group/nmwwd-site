One of the clearer findings in wrong-way crash research is that the drivers involved are frequently a long way from home. The AAA Foundation's analysis of fatal wrong-way crashes from 2014 through 2023 identified drivers living far from the crash location as an elevated-risk group, along with drivers operating vehicles registered to someone else.

Both findings point at the same underlying condition: the driver did not know the road.

## What familiarity actually provides

A local driver approaching an interchange they have used many times is not really reading it. They are recognizing it, and that is a much cheaper cognitive operation.

They know which turn leads where. They know the ramp geometry before it is visible. They know which lane to be in and when the decision point arrives. Signage functions as confirmation of something already known rather than as the primary source of information.

Strip that away and the entire burden falls on what the road communicates in the moment — at night, at speed, with one chance to get it right.

## Why visitors are in the worst position

**No mental model.** Every decision is made fresh, from signage, in the time available.

**Navigation apps compete for attention.** A driver following turn-by-turn directions is dividing attention between a screen and the road, and app instructions occasionally lag or resolve ambiguously at complex interchanges. "Turn left" is unhelpful when there are two left options and one is a ramp.

**Regional design conventions differ.** Interchange layouts, signage placement, and ramp configurations vary between states and eras of construction. A driver whose expectations were formed elsewhere can misread a locally standard design.

**Rental and borrowed vehicles add friction.** Unfamiliar controls, mirrors that are not adjusted the way you would adjust them, headlights that behave differently. Small things, but they consume attention that would otherwise go to the road.

**Fatigue and timing.** Long-distance travel concentrates arrivals at odd hours — late at night, after many hours of driving — which stacks with every other risk factor.

## The design implication

If a meaningful share of wrong-way entries involve drivers who have never seen the interchange before, then the standard for interchange design cannot be "a driver who knows this road can navigate it."

The standard has to be: **can someone who has never been here, at night, while tired, following a navigation app, reliably tell which ramp goes which way?**

That is a much harder standard, and it is the correct one. It is also achievable, and it is exactly what the countermeasures used against wrong-way entry are designed to do — larger and lower signage, wrong-way pavement arrows, red retroreflective markers, geometry that makes the wrong turn awkward rather than merely wrong.

The useful property of all of these is that none of them require prior knowledge. They work on a first-time visitor identically to a local.

## What travelers can do

The road should not depend on driver behavior, but there are things that genuinely reduce personal exposure:

- **Plan unfamiliar routes for daylight when the trip allows it.** Unfamiliarity and darkness are separately elevated risk factors, and combining them is worse than either alone.
- **Look at the route before driving it,** particularly the interchanges. Thirty seconds with a map builds a rough mental model where there was none.
- **At complex interchanges, read signage rather than following the vehicle ahead.** Following is efficient and it inherits the other driver's mistakes.
- **Stop when tired rather than pushing to arrive.** The last hour of a long drive, late at night, on unknown roads, is where several risk factors meet.
- **Slow down at interchanges you do not know.** Speed reduces the time available to read and correct, and reading is the whole task.

## Why this is a solvable problem

Familiarity cannot be distributed — people will always drive on roads they do not know, and that is a normal and good thing.

Legibility can be built. An interchange that reads correctly to a first-time visitor at 2am reads correctly to everyone, in every condition. That is the target, and it is an engineering problem rather than a behavioral one, which makes it one of the more tractable pieces of this whole subject.
