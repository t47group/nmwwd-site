The day after Thanksgiving kicks off the busiest shopping stretch of the year. Roads near malls and big-box stores fill up, parking lots turn into slow-moving puzzles, and drivers are juggling navigation, store hours, texts from family, and deal alerts all at once.

It is a good moment for a refresher on Arizona's hands-free law and why it exists.

## Why distraction is so dangerous

NHTSA reports that 3,208 people were killed in crashes involving distracted drivers in 2024, and 315,167 were injured.

The agency's best-known illustration is simple: sending or reading a text takes your eyes off the road for about five seconds. At 55 mph, that is like driving the length of a football field with your eyes closed.

In Arizona, ADOT's 2024 crash facts report found at least 8,289 drivers involved in collisions were distracted, and ADOT notes that the real number is likely higher because distraction is hard to confirm after a crash.

NHTSA also points out that distraction is not just phones. Eating, talking to passengers, adjusting the stereo, and programming navigation all count.

## What Arizona's law says

Arizona's hands-free law, A.R.S. 28-914, prohibits drivers on a street or highway from:

- **Physically holding or supporting a phone** or other portable wireless device with any part of the body.
- **Writing, sending, or reading any text-based communication,** including text messages and other written content.

The law does allow some uses, including:

- **Hands-free calls** using an earpiece, headphones, or a device worn on the wrist.
- **Voice-based communication,** such as dictating a text by voice.
- **Navigation and GPS** used in a hands-free manner.
- **Reporting illegal activity or calling for emergency help.**

The restrictions apply while the vehicle is being driven. The statute's exception for a stopped vehicle refers to being parked or stopped under specific traffic laws, not simply waiting at a red light, so the safest assumption is that the phone stays down while you are in traffic.

## The penalties

According to the current text of A.R.S. 28-914:

- **First violation:** a civil penalty of at least $75 and not more than $149.
- **Second or later violation:** $400.
- **An additional $150** if a violation results in a crash involving a motorcycle.

This is general information, not legal advice. Check the current statute or consult an attorney for specifics.

## Why it matters for wrong-way safety

The most useful thing a driver can do when facing a wrong-way vehicle is react early: ease off the gas, steer away when no one is beside you, and get to a safe place. ADOT reports that three out of four wrong-way crashes happen between 6 p.m. and 6 a.m., when headlights may be the first and only warning.

A driver looking at a phone loses exactly the seconds that make that reaction possible. The same is true for spotting a red WRONG WAY sign at an unfamiliar ramp, a pedestrian crossing a dark street, or brake lights ahead.

## Habits for the holiday season

**Set navigation before you shift into drive.** Program the route, mount the phone, and let voice directions do the work.

**Use Do Not Disturb while driving.** Most phones can silence notifications automatically when you are in the car.

**Let a passenger handle the phone.** Directions, texts, and store hours can all be someone else's job.

**Pull over to deal with it.** If something cannot wait, find a safe place to park first.

**Slow down in parking lots.** Shopping center lots are full of people walking between cars, children, and drivers backing out. Give yourself time to see them.

**Do not text someone you know is driving.** If a family member is on the road, wait for them to arrive.

## Small decisions, big margins

Distracted driving rarely feels dangerous in the moment. It feels like a quick glance. The problem is that the moment something goes wrong on the road is not the moment you get to choose.
