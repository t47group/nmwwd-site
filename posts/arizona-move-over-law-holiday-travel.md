Thanksgiving week is one of the busiest stretches of the year on American roads. AAA projected that 81.8 million people would travel at least 50 miles from home over the 2025 Thanksgiving holiday, a record, and that at least 73 million of them would go by car.

More traffic means more breakdowns, more crashes, more tow trucks, and more troopers working on the side of the road. That makes this a good week to review a law many drivers only half remember.

## What the law requires

Arizona's Move Over law, A.R.S. 28-775, covers two different situations.

**When an emergency vehicle approaches** with lights and siren, drivers must pull to the right edge of the road, clear of intersections, and stop until it passes.

**When you approach a stationary vehicle** displaying flashing or warning lights on the road or shoulder, the law requires you to move over into a lane that is not next to the stopped vehicle, if you can do so safely on a road with enough lanes. If you cannot safely change lanes, you must slow down and proceed with caution at a speed that is safe for the conditions.

## It is not just for first responders

ADOT has emphasized that the stationary-vehicle rule covers any stopped vehicle with its hazard lights on. That includes:

- Police and fire vehicles
- Tow trucks
- ADOT crews and other highway workers
- Stranded motorists with a flat tire or a dead engine

That last group is the one many drivers forget. A family on the shoulder changing a tire on the way to Thanksgiving dinner is protected by the same law as a trooper at a crash scene.

## The penalties

A 2021 change to the law set civil penalties for violating the stationary-vehicle rule:

- **$275** for a first violation
- **$500** for a second violation within five years
- **$1,000** for a third or later violation within five years

The same change formally named the statute the "Arizona Move Over Law" and required ADOT to educate the public about it.

## Why it matters at night

With sunset now before 5:30 p.m. in Phoenix, a lot of holiday driving happens after dark. ADOT's 2024 crash facts report counted 585 people killed in crashes in dark conditions that year, close to half of all traffic deaths in the state.

At night, a vehicle stopped on the shoulder is harder to judge. Drivers see flashing lights but have a harder time telling how far away they are and how close the vehicle sits to the travel lane. Moving over early, instead of at the last second, gives everyone more margin.

Impaired drivers are a particular danger to anyone stopped on the shoulder. They are more likely to drift, and less likely to notice and react to flashing lights in time. It is one more reason the plan for a sober ride home matters so much during the holidays.

## If you are the one who breaks down

The other side of the Move Over law is what to do when you are the stopped vehicle. A few general steps help:

- **Get as far off the road as you can.** If the car is still moving, try to reach an exit or a wide shoulder.
- **Turn on your hazard lights** right away so other drivers know to move over.
- **Stay buckled in your vehicle** in most cases, rather than standing on the shoulder next to traffic.
- **Call for help.** If you are in a dangerous spot on a freeway, call 911.
- **Know your location.** Note the highway, direction, nearest exit, or milepost before you call.

## Before your holiday trip

- Check your tires, including the spare, before a long drive.
- Keep your phone charged and a charger in the car.
- Carry water, especially on long desert stretches.
- Know the route well enough to describe where you are if you have to call for help.

## Give them room

Moving over takes a few seconds. For the people standing on the shoulder, whether they are wearing a badge, driving a tow truck, or simply trying to get their family back on the road, those few feet can make all the difference.
