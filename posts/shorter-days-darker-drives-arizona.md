Arizona does not change its clocks, so the shift into darker evenings happens quietly here. There is no single Sunday when the commute suddenly flips. But by early November it has happened anyway.

According to the U.S. Naval Observatory, the sun sets in Phoenix at 5:32 p.m. on November 6 and at 5:23 p.m. by November 20. Civil twilight, the last useful daylight, ends less than half an hour after that. By early December, sunset is around 5:20 p.m.

That means a large share of the Valley's after-work driving, school pickups from evening activities, and holiday errands now happens in the dark.

## What the Arizona crash data shows

ADOT's 2024 Arizona Motor Vehicle Crash Facts report gives a clear picture of how much darkness matters on Arizona roads:

- **585 people were killed in crashes in dark conditions** in 2024, close to half of the 1,228 traffic deaths statewide that year.
- **The peak hour for fatal crashes was 6 to 7 p.m.**, exactly the hour that has now moved from daylight to darkness.

Darkness also lines up with the conditions where wrong-way crashes happen. ADOT reports that three out of four wrong-way crashes occur between 6 p.m. and 6 a.m. The AAA Foundation for Traffic Safety's national study of fatal wrong-way crashes on divided highways from 2014 through 2023 found elevated risk in darkness and at dawn and dusk.

None of that changes the road. It changes what drivers can see on it.

## Why the same road feels different at night

**You see less, and later.** Headlights only reach so far. Signs, lane markings, and other vehicles appear later than they do in daylight, which leaves less time to react.

**Interchanges are harder to read.** In the dark, an on-ramp and an off-ramp can look like the same pair of lanes curving toward the freeway. Drivers depend more on signs and reflectors and less on the overall shape of the road.

**Dusk is a transition, not a moment.** The half hour around sunset combines low sun, long shadows, and eyes that are still adjusting. Phoenix's east-west streets can put the setting sun directly in a driver's eyes just before it disappears.

**Pedestrians become harder to see.** People walking near the road at dusk and after dark are easy to miss, especially away from intersections.

**Fatigue arrives with the dark.** An evening drive after a full workday is a different drive from the morning one, even on the same route.

## Simple adjustments that help

**Clean the glass.** A hazy windshield scatters headlights and low sun. Clean the inside as well as the outside, and replace worn wipers before winter storms.

**Check your headlights.** Make sure both work, that the lenses are clear, and that they are aimed correctly. Cloudy lenses can noticeably reduce how far you see.

**Use your lights early.** Turn headlights on as the light fades, not when it is fully dark. It helps others see you during dusk, when it is easy to blend into the fading light.

**Dim the dashboard.** A bright screen inside the car makes it harder for your eyes to adjust to the road outside.

**Slow down where you cannot see far.** Drive at a speed that lets you stop within the distance your headlights show you.

**Keep your sunglasses and visor handy for the last hour of daylight** and take them off as soon as the sun drops.

**Read the signs at every ramp.** Especially on unfamiliar routes, look for the red DO NOT ENTER and WRONG WAY signs and the red reflectors that tell a driver they are pointed the wrong way. If you see them facing you, stop.

## If headlights come at you

Darkness is when you are most likely to see a wrong-way driver, and it is also when it is hardest to judge what is happening. ADOT's guidance is straightforward:

- Ease off the gas and slow down.
- Make sure no vehicle is beside you, then steer away from the wrong-way driver.
- Get to a safe place, call 911, and report it.
- If an overhead sign warns of a wrong-way driver, move toward the nearest exit as soon as it is safe.

## A seasonal reset

The change in daylight is gradual enough that many drivers never consciously adjust to it. This week is a good time to do it on purpose: clean the windshield, check the lights, and leave a little more margin on the drive home.
